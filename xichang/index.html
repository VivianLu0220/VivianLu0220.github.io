0 · Overview: one paragraph, one pipeline, one division of work

The project in one paragraph

Xichang is a city of 950,000 people. In 2019 it had 19,990 police-reported crashes. In Phase 1 (2023) I did three things in ArcGIS Pro: kernel density estimation (KDE), Gi* hot spots, and colocation quotients (CLQ). CLQ tests whether crash types and land uses occur together. I also helped interpret the team's LightGBM model with SHAP. The maps showed where crashes happened. They could not show why. The reason was missing traffic volumes. Without volumes, "many crashes" and "many vehicles" look the same. In Phase 2 (2026) I built a sketch-level four-step travel demand model. It has 52 + 14 zones. I built it in Python with AequilibraE. I replicated it in TransCAD, with R² 0.9999. I back-cast the model to 2019. I used vehicle-kilometers traveled (VKT) as exposure. Then I redid HSM-style screening with a safety performance function (SPF) and Empirical Bayes (EB). The conclusion reversed. Hangtian Avenue ranks first by crash count but 48th by EB excess crashes. The roads with real excess are the Wuda Line and Heyan Road Section 2. The through-arterial risk ratio fell from 2.68 to 1.22. It is no longer significant. Finally, I linked the macro and micro levels with 2035 scenarios and VISSIM/SSAM. A 5-point mode shift offsets about half of the crash increase. A protected left turn cuts crossing conflicts at the worst intersection by 79%.

Limitations
  • Call records cover far more than official crash statistics. They include many minor property-damage-only crashes. "Casualties" means persons injured. It cannot be converted to fatalities.
  • Spatial analysis is biased toward points near landmarks. Intersection rankings should be read as rankings of "crashes that could be located to an intersection".
  • I did not keep a spot-check accuracy for the text extraction. I did not record the geocoding failure rate.
  • The raw micro data were not saved after the project closed. In 2026 I could only recompute from four things: summary tables, the 9,384 points in the geodatabase, the LCLQ results and the 28,847 POIs. LightGBM and DTW clustering cannot be re-run.

2 · Who gets hurt and when: three counterintuitive results

Descriptive statistics are more than bar charts. All three results in this section depend on choosing the right exposure, that is, the right denominator. For severity I use casualties per crash. For weather I use the number of days. Day versus night has no exposure measure. That is a limitation.

2.1 Participant pairs and severity

Method

The data have no injury severity grade. So I use casualties per crash as the severity measure K/N. k is the number of casualties and n is the number of crashes. For the 95% confidence interval of the rate k/n I use the Garwood exact Poisson interval:

[ χ²0.025(2k) / 2n ,　χ²0.975(2k+2) / 2n ]

What I did and results

I built a 6 × 6 matrix of participant pairs. The matrix is symmetric, so I use only the upper triangle. For each cell I computed k/n and its confidence interval.

  • Motor vehicle × motor vehicle: 11,407 crashes (57%). Only 0.06 casualties per crash.
  • The most dangerous pair is heavy vehicle × pedestrian: 70 crashes, 143 casualties. That is 2.04 per crash (CI 1.72–2.41), 8 times the city mean of 0.26. Next come pedestrian × three-wheeler 1.11, pedestrian × two-wheeler 1.07 and motor vehicle × pedestrian 1.04. Motor vehicle × two-wheeler has 0.66 per crash, but the count is large. It has 1,746 casualties, one third of the total.
  • Crashes involving vulnerable road users: 5,223 crashes (26.1%) → 4,050 casualties (79.1%), 0.78 per crash. Other crashes: 14,767 crashes → 1,072 casualties, 0.07 per crash. The ratio is 10.7 times. Every pair with a vulnerable road user is significantly above the mean. Every motor-vehicle-only pair is significantly below it. The confidence intervals of the two groups do not overlap.

Economic reading: in an asymmetric collision, the heavier party causes almost all of the marginal external cost (Lindberg 2001). The lighter party bears that cost. So corrective policy should target the heavier party: speed limits, access restrictions, right-of-way and physical separation. Policy should not rest on "safety education" for vulnerable users.

2.2 Weather: exposure confounding and risk compensation

Method

Comparing total crashes across weather types is wrong. The totals are confounded by the number of days. I use the number of days with each weather as exposure. Then I compute the daily crash rate and the rate ratio relative to sunny days:

RR = (a / ta) / (b / tb)　　se(ln RR) = √(1/a + 1/b)　　95% CI = exp(ln RR ± 1.96 · se) This is a log-scale Wald interval under a Poisson assumption.

The 2023 report said "light rain has the most crashes (8,155, 41%)". That is classic exposure confounding. There were 151 light-rain days, more than the 135 sunny days. After adjusting for days, light-rain and moderate-rain days have crash rates 5–6% lower than sunny days. Only heavy rain is significantly higher. This fits risk compensation (Peltzman 1975). Drivers can perceive mild surface risk. They slow down, keep longer gaps and travel less. This offsets the risk. Heavy rain cuts visibility and floods the road surface. That is beyond what drivers can compensate for.

Planning implication: rainy-day safety campaigns have low marginal benefit. It is more effective to reduce physical risk during heavy rain. Examples: drainage, skid-resistant pavement, signs at flood-prone spots and arterial traffic control during storm warnings.

2.3 Day/night, seasonal and hourly patterns

  • Day/night: 15,891 crashes by day and 4,099 at night (20.5%). The night share barely changes across the five weather types (19–21%). So weather and day/night effects are additive, with no interaction. Split into two 12-hour halves, the daytime crash rate is 3.9 times the night rate. Most of this gap comes from exposure. Night traffic is usually only 15–25% of daytime traffic. I have no hourly traffic volumes. So I cannot assess the marginal benefit of streetlights.
  • Seasonal: January–June is low and flat. Crashes rise from July and stay high in August because of summer tourism. They rise again in October. They peak in December, when the Anning River valley has frequent winter fog.
  • Hourly: very few crashes from 0–6 h. A sharp rise at 7 h. Twin peaks at 8 h and 18 h. A decline after 20 h.
DTW clustering (original 2023 analysis, cannot be re-run): three "crash rhythms" of road segments

Method: DTW (dynamic time warping) is a distance measure. It lets two time series stretch along the time axis to align. So it tolerates shifted peaks. Each segment has a 168-hour (one-week) crash series. I computed pairwise DTW distances and ran k-medoids clustering. Two measures together set the number of clusters k: the mean DTW distance and the silhouette coefficient S = (b − a) / max(a, b). a is the mean distance to other points in the same cluster. b is the mean distance to the nearest other cluster.

Results: k = 3. The three clusters match three crash density levels. Sparse segments (< 2.5 crashes per week on average) show no daily pattern and look close to random. Medium segments (< 5) have only an evening peak. Dense segments (< 10) have morning and evening peaks, and night crashes are not zero. Implication: safety spending on dense segments can be scheduled by time of day, such as peak signal timing and enforcement. On sparse segments it can only be allocated by place, such as engineering measures.

In brief

"Vulnerable road users were in 26% of crashes but took 79% of the injuries. Rain looked dangerous by raw counts. Then I divided crashes by the number of days with each weather. Light and moderate rain had lower crash rates than clear days. This is consistent with risk compensation. Only heavy rain was significantly higher. Throughout, I used Garwood Poisson intervals and rate ratios, not raw counts."

LimitationsEach crash counts two participants, so participant shares sum to more than 100%. The data have no age, helmet or speed information. There were only 5 heavy-rain days. The weather table covers 361 days. Day versus night has no exposure measure. The DTW and hourly figures come directly from the 2023 version.

The spatial analysis has three layers. The first is a continuous surface: kernel density estimation (KDE) shows where crashes concentrate. The second is grid statistics. Gi* and Moran's I test whether the clustering is significant. The Lorenz curve measures how concentrated it is. The third is the road network: I snapped crash points to OSM segments and intersections. Segments and intersections are the units that engineers can act on.

3.1 Kernel density estimation (KDE)

Method

Place a smooth "kernel", like a small hill, on each crash point. Add up all the hills to get a continuous density surface:

f(s) = 1 / (n h²) · Σi K( ‖s − si‖ / h ) h is the bandwidth. If it is too small, the surface is noisy, with one small hill per point. If it is too large, hot spots are smoothed away. In this project I first counted points on a 100 m grid. Then I smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of σ = 250 m. Finally I divided by area to get crashes/km². For bandwidths of 150–400 m the hot spot locations do not change. Bandwidth affects only the peak height.

2023 vs 2026

  • 2023: ArcGIS Pro Kernel Density tool with a search radius of 0.01. The coordinate system was WGS84, so this meant 0.01 degree ≈ 1.1 km. The output unit was "per square degree", which has no physical meaning. That is why the 2023 report showed peaks such as 273,500.
  • 2026: project to UTM 48N first. Use a 250 m Gaussian kernel on a 100 m grid. Density is in crashes/km². Each crash type has its own color scale.

Results

All three crash types share one core. The core is the old commercial district bounded by Chang'an Middle Road, Chang'an South Road, Hangtian Avenue Section 1 and Sanchakou South Road. It also includes the corridor along Chang'an Road, east to the East Coach Station and west to the West Coach Station. Peak densities: angle > 300 crashes/km², rear-end about 180, rollover only 25. Angle and rear-end crashes stretch along arterials and cluster at intersections. Rollovers are more dispersed. Outside the city they form separate clusters on the G5 Jingjiu section and the G348 Heyan section. The cause is curves and grades.

3.2 How concentrated: Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient

I sorted the 250 m grid cells with crashes in descending order of crash count. Then I plotted the cumulative curve. All three types together have 830 cells with crashes. The densest 5% of cells hold 32% of crashes, 10% hold 48% and 20% hold 67%. The Gini is 0.64 for all types combined. By type: angle 0.59, rear-end 0.57, rollover 0.41.

Reading the marginal-return curve: covering the top 10% of cells reaches almost half of all crashes. These are about 80 cells, about 5 km². The next 10% of cells adds only 19 percentage points. Each further 10% adds fewer than 10 points. This is the direct basis for the proposal of a 30 km/h zone in the core.

Limitation: the modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP). Cell size changes the Gini and the correlation coefficients. For cells of 100–500 m the direction of the results is stable. But I did not report a systematic sensitivity test.

3.3 Is the clustering significant? Moran's I and Getis-Ord Gi*

Global Moran's I (Moran 1948)

I = (n / S0) · ΣiΣj wij zi zj / Σi zi²　　zi = xi − x̄,　S0 = ΣiΣj wij I > 0 means positive spatial autocorrelation: similar values cluster. I < 0 means negative autocorrelation. I ≈ 0 means random. Significance comes from a permutation test. I shuffled the values across cells 999 times. Then I checked where the observed I falls in the permutation distribution.

Local Getis-Ord Gi* (Getis & Ord 1992)

Gi* = ( Σj∈Ni xj − x̄ |Ni| ) / ( S · √[(n|Ni| − |Ni|²) / (n − 1)] )　　S = √( Σj xj² / n − x̄² ) It is computed for cell i and its neighborhood. The neighborhood includes the cell itself; queen contiguity = a 3 × 3 window. Under the null hypothesis the statistic is approximately standard normal. |Gi*| > 1.645 / 1.96 / 2.576 corresponds to 90 / 95 / 99% confidence. Positive values are hot spots. Negative values are cold spots.

Gi* versus KDE: KDE is a density. It only says "there are many here". Gi* is a statistic. It says "this cell and its neighbors are high together", and too high to be random. Gi* measures how far the neighborhood sum departs from the global mean. So an isolated high cell is not necessarily a hot spot. A connected area of moderately high cells can be one.

What I did and results

The central city has 1,188 cells of 250 m. Gi* uses a 3 × 3 convolution. Moran's I uses queen contiguity without the cell itself and 999 permutations. Result: Moran's I = 0.29 (p = 0.001). There are 156 hot-spot cells at 95% or above, 128 of them at 99%. They are 13% of cells but hold 65% of crashes (3,764). The hot spots form a continuous "hot belt": West Coach Station → Chang'an Road → old commercial district → East Coach Station → southeast along Hangtian Avenue. There are no significant cold spots.

Limitation: multiple comparisonsArcGIS Hot Spot Analysis offers an FDR (false discovery rate) correction. FDR correction controls the inflation of false positives from multiple testing and spatial dependence. My rework script did not apply FDR correction. It classified cells directly by z thresholds. Spatial autocorrelation makes standard significance tests too optimistic.

3.4 Network screening: snapping to OSM segments and intersections

Why: engineering measures are applied to roads and intersections. Grid hot spots are not something engineers can act on. So I snapped each crash point to the nearest segment. Then I summed crashes and crashes per km by road name. For crashes per km, the exposure is the total OSM length of all segments with that name. An intersection is a node with degree ≥ 3. Crashes within 30 m of an intersection count as "intersection-related".

How: I used osmnx to get 3,424 drive segments in the core, plus 2,744 national/provincial highway and arterial segments city-wide. I projected them to UTM 48N. Snapping used geopandas.sjoin_nearest(max_distance=60). Road names came from the OSM name field.

Results

  • The top 15 roads are 8.7% of road length but carry 33% of matched crashes. By crash count: Hangtian Avenue 534 (9 per km), Wuda Line 288, First Ring Road 218, Heyan Road Section 2 206, Haibin Middle Road 159. But the highest per km are short streets in the old town: Chang'an Road 88 per km, Chang'an East Road 70, Chuncheng Road 55, Chang'an Middle Road 53.
  • By OSM class: trunk 16.9 per km, secondary 13.4, tertiary 8.2, primary only 6.0. Density does not rise steadily with road class.
  • The top three roads for rollovers are all through arterials: Fengxiangkou Bridge section 33, Wuda Line 28, Hangtian Avenue 27.
  • Intersections: Hangtian Avenue × Shengli South Road, 77 crashes, ranks first, with 42 angle and 33 rear-end. Second place has only 36. The top 15 intersections hold 47% of crashes that could be located to an intersection.

Limitations: the network is from 2026. The 12.9% intersection-related share comes from how addresses were geocoded. Roads that share a name dilute the per-km density. For example, the merged OSM length of Hangtian Avenue is 58.6 km.

3.5 Built environment: POI correlation, negative binomial residuals, hub distance bands

Negative binomial regression: method

Crash counts are overdispersed: the variance exceeds the mean. So a Poisson model does not fit. I used a negative binomial model:

E[crashi] = μi = exp(β0 + β1 ln(1 + POIi))　　Var = μ + α μ²
Pearson residual zi = (yi − μ̂i) / √(μ̂i + α μ̂i²) β1 is a log elasticity: 1% more POIs → β1% more crashes. Cells with z > 2 are marked "significantly above what activity predicts". This is the "risk" term of the identity, not the "exposure" term.

Results

  • On a 500 m grid, Spearman ρ = 0.82. The densest quintile has 87 crashes per cell. The sparsest has 1.7.
  • But crashes per unit of activity fall as density rises: crashes per 100 POIs drop from 113 in Q1 to 23 in Q5. Density is a positive externality for safety, because speeds are lower. Total activity hides this effect.
  • NB results: β1 = 0.65 (s.e. 0.04), α = 2.0. There are 33 excess cells with z > 2. They have 1,484 observed crashes against 204 expected. The top 8 excess cells are almost all on through arterials: Wuda Line, Dangui Bridge, First Ring Road and Hangtian Avenue. One Wuda Line cell has 77 crashes, 2 POIs, 2.2 expected and z = 21.3.
  • Hubs: within 1 km of the coach stations, density is 10 times that of the 2–3 km band. But crashes per 100 POIs are flat across bands (25–35). So activity intensity fully explains the high density near coach stations. The rail station is different. In the 0–0.5 km band there are 52 crashes per 100 POIs. This falls steadily to 27 farther out. So the station forecourt area carries its own risk. The cause is taxis, three-wheelers, pedestrians and pick-up/drop-off cars mixing.
This finding was later narrowedFrom 2023 to early 2026 the reading was: through arterials exceed what activity predicts, so the roads themselves carry risk. But POIs cannot see through traffic. In Phase 2, after adding vehicle-kilometers traveled (VKT), "through arterial" as a class is no longer significant. Only a few specific locations still show excess crashes. See Section 11.

4 · Colocation quotient (CLQ / LCLQ)My own work

The question: "Do angle crashes happen more often next to shopping centers?" Counting POIs inside a buffer does not answer it, because shopping POIs are common everywhere. The CLQ divides "the share of B near A" by "the share of B citywide". This controls for how many B points exist. The CLQ is asymmetric. It has a local version and a permutation test.

Global CLQ

This card gives the definition and the sources. Leslie & Kronenfeld (2011) proposed the global CLQ. Cromley, Hanink & Bentley (2014) proposed a geographically weighted version. Wang et al. (2017) proposed the local CLQ with a permutation test. The ArcGIS Pro "Colocation Analysis" tool is based on the Wang version.

CLQA→B = ( CA→B / NA ) / ( N'B / (N − 1) )　　CA→B = Σi∈A Σj≠i wij · 1[j ∈ B] NA and NB are the point counts of the two categories. N is the total point count. N'B = NB (if A ≠ B) or NB − 1 (if A = B), because a point cannot be its own neighbor. The numerator is the observed share of B in the neighborhoods of A points. The denominator is the expected share of B under randomness. CLQ > 1 means colocation. CLQ < 1 means isolation. CLQ = 1 means random. The CLQ is asymmetric: CLQA→B ≠ CLQB→A.

Local LCLQ (one value per A point)

LCLQAi→B = NAi→B / ( N'B / (N − 1) )　　NAi→B = Σj wij fij / Σj wij
wij = exp( −0.5 · dij² / dib² )　(Gaussian kernel; dib is the bandwidth distance of point i) fij = 1 if j ∈ B, otherwise 0. Near neighbors get large weights. The weight falls close to zero at the bandwidth.

How the ArcGIS Pro tool works

  1. Neighborhood: K nearest neighbors (default 8), a distance band, or a spatial weights matrix.
  2. Kernel: with a Gaussian kernel, weights decay with distance. A box kernel gives equal weights.
  3. Significance: a local permutation test, 99 permutations by default. Each permutation shuffles the B labels and recomputes the LCLQ. This gives the p-value.
  4. Output fields: LCLQ, p-value, LCLQTYPE. LCLQTYPE has five classes: Colocated–Significant, Colocated–Not Significant, Isolated–Significant, Isolated–Not Significant, Undefined. Undefined means no B point falls in the neighborhood, so the LCLQ cannot be computed. The global CLQ table also gives a p-value for each pair of categories.

Terminology: the 2023 Chinese report called this measure "location entropy". The defense reviewers pointed out that it is a quotient, not an entropy. The rebuilt report uses "colocation quotient (CLQ)" throughout.

5.1 How GBDT and LightGBM work

Gradient boosted decision trees (GBDT): the prediction is the sum of T regression trees, f(x) = Σt ht(x). Each new tree fits the negative gradient of the current loss, that is, the residual. The trees "correct errors" one after another.

LightGBM (Ke et al., NIPS 2017, Microsoft) is faster and uses less memory than XGBoost. It handles categorical variables natively and is robust to collinearity. It relies on three techniques:

  1. GOSS (gradient-based one-side sampling): keep the top a × 100% of samples by absolute gradient. Randomly draw b × (1 − a) × 100% of the rest, and scale their weights up by (1 − a)/b. A large gradient means the current model fits poorly, so the sample carries more information. These samples must be kept.
  2. EFB (exclusive feature bundling): bundle mutually exclusive sparse features into one feature. Mutually exclusive means they are never nonzero at the same time. This reduces dimensionality.
  3. Histogram algorithm + leaf-wise growth: bin continuous features. Grow the tree at the leaf with the largest gain, not level by level.

Why this model: segment crash counts are overdispersed counts. The features are highly collinear, for example lane count and road class, or office density and night lights. The features also have thresholds and interactions. GBDT handles nonlinearity and interactions automatically and is robust to collinearity. Its weakness is interpretability, so it is paired with SHAP.

5.2 How SHAP works

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations, Lundberg & Lee 2017) splits each prediction into additive contributions:

ŷi = φ0 + Σj φij　　(φ0 = mean prediction over all samples)
φj = ΣS ⊆ F∖{j} [ |S|! (|F| − |S| − 1)! / |F|! ] · [ fS∪{j}(x) − fS(x) ] φj is the game-theoretic Shapley value (Shapley 1953). It is the average marginal contribution of feature j over all possible joining orders. It satisfies four properties: efficiency (the terms sum to the prediction minus the mean), symmetry, dummy, and additivity. Exact computation is exponential, 2|F|. TreeExplainer (Lundberg et al. 2020) computes it exactly for tree models in polynomial time.

Intuition: think of the 13 features as 13 teammates. Together they "produce" the crash count of a segment. A feature's SHAP value is how many crashes it contributes on average, positive or negative. The average runs over all joining orders, so it is fair.

5.4 Method and results in this project

Data: 2,847 segments. The dependent variable is the segment crash count (a count, not a rate). The 13 features fall into three groups. Land use: office / commercial / residential POI counts, WorldPop population density, night lights. Hubs: distance to the railway station / airport / long-distance bus station. Road: administrative class, lane count, road form, median type, direction of flow.

Mean |SHAP| falls into three tiers. Tier 1, > 4: office land use 7.3, railway station distance 4.5, administrative class 4.4, lane count 4.2. Tier 2, 2.5–3.5: commercial, airport distance, residential, population density, bus station distance, night lights. Tier 3, < 1.6: road form, median type, direction of flow.

Direction: all land-use features are positive. All hub distances are negative: nearer means more crashes. Higher class and more lanes mean more crashes. Segments with a physical median have almost all negative SHAP. It is the only road attribute in the model that lowers crashes systematically.

Seven patterns in the dependence plots

  1. Below 50 office POIs, SHAP ≈ 0. Above 50 it rises roughly exponentially. The densest segments reach +40 to +50 crashes. This is a threshold effect.
  2. Residential density has a kink at about 100. It interacts with road class: at the same density, higher-class roads have more crashes.
  3. Night lights below 20 give all-negative SHAP. Above 50, SHAP rises clearly.
  4. Commercial land use is nonlinear but rises overall. Where commercial and office uses are both dense, SHAP is clearly higher.
  5. Hub effects are local. SHAP is positive and volatile within 3 km of the railway station, 5–7 km of the airport, and 1–2 km of the bus station. Beyond that it returns to zero. Segments near hubs are also dense commercial segments. Hubs act through the activity around them.
  6. ≤ 3 lanes: all negative. ≥ 4 lanes: all positive. ≥ 8 lanes: another jump. This is the cleanest design signal: "wide roads are more dangerous", in the sense of crash burden.
  7. Divided two-way roads have the highest SHAP in economically active areas. Frontage roads are also in active areas, but they contribute very little. This suggests that separating through traffic from local traffic works in itself.

Economic reading: land use and hubs act mainly through exposure. Road class and lane count act on both exposure (more vehicles) and risk (higher design speed, more complex lane changes). This is risk compensation in reverse: "the safer the road looks, the faster people drive". A physical median does not depend on driver discipline. It is central to the Safe System approach. International evidence: physical medians on urban arterials can cut injury crashes by 20–40%. The office threshold effect suggests that land-use approvals should include a traffic impact assessment to internalize the externality.

The most important limitations of this section
  • The model explains crash counts, not crash rates. We had no segment traffic volumes. Any feature that raises traffic volume acts through both exposure and risk. The model cannot separate the two channels. The Phase 2 SPF gives indirect evidence: within a road class, the elasticity of crashes with respect to VKT is only 0.00–0.45. So much of "higher-class roads have more crashes" is traffic volume.
  • Correlation is not causation. The direction of SHAP is more reliable than its size. Unobserved traffic volume, intersection density and speed limits may confound the results.
  • The final project report gave no accuracy metrics (no RMSE / R², no description of a train/test split). The hyperparameters were not kept. The raw data were lost, so the model cannot be retrained. The figures are the 2023 originals.
  • Division of work: my teammate trained the model. I took part in interpreting the features. In 2026 I reinterpreted the results on my own.

5.5 Bayesian network (2023 method, not finished)

Principle

Bayes' theorem: P(A|B) = P(B|A)·P(A) / P(B). A Bayesian network is a directed acyclic graph (DAG). Nodes = random variables, edges = conditional dependence, no edge = conditional independence. The joint distribution is P(X1…Xn) = Πi P(Xi | parents(Xi)). Experts can set the structure, or it can be learned from data. Learning methods come in two kinds: score-based (such as BIC / K2) and constraint-based (such as PC). The parameters are conditional probability tables (CPTs), learned from data by EM or maximum likelihood. Strengths: it handles uncertainty and missing data, and it supports scenario inference. Example: "with rain + night + poor street lighting, what is the probability of a severe crash?"

Mutual information is used for feature selection: I(X;Y) = Σ p(x,y) log[p(x,y) / (p(x)p(y))] = H(Y) − H(Y|X). It measures how much knowing X reduces the uncertainty about Y.

How far this project got

The tool was GeNIe. In 2023 the team first discretized the variables and computed marginal distributions (N = 19,990). Severity: minor 77% / moderate 20% / severe 3%. Visibility: good 41 / fair 34 / poor 25. Street lighting: good 59 / fair 26 / poor 14. Day 79 / night 21. Weather: clear 38 / cloudy 13 / light rain 41 / moderate rain 6 / heavy rain 2. Road surface: dry 51 / wet 49. The network structure came from the literature and experience: weather → road surface, visibility; lighting + street lights → visibility; participants → collision type; road surface + visibility + collision type → severity. Parameter learning and validation were not completed.

Only descriptive conclusions are possible. Severity is highly skewed: 3% ≈ 600 "severe" crashes, almost all in combinations of vulnerable road users (VRU) and heavy vehicles. "Poor" visibility is 25%, while night is only 21%. So poor visibility also comes from rain and fog. Most of the variance in severity comes from "who hit whom", not from weather or lighting.

LimitationsThe rebuilt report no longer presents the Bayesian network as a finished model. It keeps only the marginal distribution table and the structure diagram. Future work could model severity with a Bayesian network or an ordered logit. Police-reported crash data give limited support for severity modeling: there is no injury scale, and "severe" is inferred from casualty counts and keywords. Whether a crash is reported is itself related to severity, and minor crashes are underreported. This is selection bias.

6 · Economics framework: one identity, three concepts, seven recommendations

In the 2026 rebuild I replaced the 2023 framework. The old framework was "feature analysis + identification of contributing factors". The new one is an identity from road safety economics. The identity itself adds no new information. Its value is that it maps each policy tool to one dimension. It also explains one point: cutting severity where crashes already concentrate gives the highest return.

The marginal benefit of an intervention = the term it changes × the other three terms. Hot-spot corridors already have high exposure and high risk. Cutting severity there gives the largest product.

Three economic concepts

Crash externality
When drivers choose speed and attention, they weigh only their own time and risk. They do not count the risk they impose on pedestrians and cyclists (Vickrey 1968; Lindberg 2001). Edlin & Karaca-Mandic (2006) estimate that in high-density states, each extra driver imposes > $2,000 per year in external costs on others. Implication: the market supplies too little safety. Prices, regulation or the road environment (Safe System) must fill the gap.
Risk compensation
Peltzman (1975): once drivers have protection such as seat belts, they drive more riskily and partly offset the benefit. Wilde's (1982) "risk homeostasis" is the extreme version. The extreme version has been refuted, but partial compensation is supported again and again. This project uses it to explain two things: crash rates on light and moderate rain days are lower than on clear days, while in heavy rain compensation is not enough; and wider, higher-class roads induce higher speeds.
Agglomeration and diminishing returns
Crashes are a by-product of travel. Travel concentrates in jobs, commerce and hubs, so the crash distribution is bound to be skewed. The upside: investing in a few places covers most of the risk (10% of grid cells → 48% of crashes). The trap: a high crash count often reflects high exposure, not high risk (Hauer 1997; HSM 2010).

Severity and speed

Collision energy ∝ v². A pedestrian hit at 30 km/h has about a 10% fatality risk. At 50 km/h the risk is > 80% (Elvik 2013; ITF 2016). Nilsson's power model: injury crashes ∝ v2, fatal crashes ∝ v4. A 10% speed cut → about −19% injury crashes. A 30 km/h limit in VRU-dense areas changes both "whether a crash happens" and "how severe it is". It is the only measure that does both.

Social cost scenario estimate

Principle: crash cost = injury cost + property damage and administrative cost + indirect costs such as congestion (Elvik 2009; Viscusi & Aldy 2003). Injury cost covers VSL, medical care, lost work and pain. VSL is the value of a statistical life, estimated from willingness-to-pay studies. China has no official VSL standard. Values in the literature range from several hundred thousand to several million yuan.

Paths to internalization and seven recommendations

There are three kinds of internalization tools. Prices: insurance premiums tied to exposure or risk, distance-based charges, truck entry fees. Prices are optimal in theory but hardest to apply at the local level. Regulation: speed limits, truck restrictions, right of way. Regulation depends on enforcement. Environment: narrower lanes, physical medians, raised crossings, tighter intersections. The environment needs one investment and works for a long time. It is the core of the Safe System approach. A fourth tool acts on future externalities: traffic impact assessment in land-use approvals. The seven recommendations are ranked by expected crash cost avoided / implementation cost. Effect sizes come from international handbooks.

  1. Make the high-density core cells (top 10%, about 5 km²) a 30 km/h zone, with raised crossings, tighter intersections and refuge islands. Basis: vulnerable road users account for 79% of casualties; Gi* hot-spot cells (13% of cells) hold 65% of crashes. Effect: speed −10% → injury crashes −19%.
  2. Heavy-vehicle management and station-area traffic organization: truck time windows and routes in the core, right-turn blind-spot treatment, and pedestrian–vehicle separation in front of the railway station. Basis: heavy vehicle × pedestrian, 2.04 casualties per crash; within 0.5 km of the railway station, crashes per unit of activity are twice the city average.
  3. Physical medians, access management and speed limits on through arterials; intersection channelization on secondary roads in the old city. Basis: a physical median is the only attribute in the model that lowers crashes systematically. Revised after Phase 2: invest first in the Wuda Line, Heyan Road Section 2 and the Chang'an Road family, not in the full length of Hangtian Avenue. Effect: injury crashes −20–40%.
  4. Add traffic safety assessment and development conditions to land-use approvals. Basis: office density shows a threshold effect; crashes concentrate within 1–3 km of hubs.
  5. Treat physical risk under heavy rain: drainage, skid-resistant pavement, signs at ponding points, and arterial control during rainstorm warnings. Basis: heavy rain RR 1.15 (1.03–1.29).
  6. Curve and grade engineering on the rollover corridors (G5 Jingjiu section, G348 Heyan section): speed limits, roadside barriers, markings. Basis: rollover self-colocation CLQ 8.63; rollovers cluster on their own outside the city.
  7. Build the exposure denominator: traffic volumes, an injury severity scale, and geocoded police reports. Use EB for network screening. Phase 2 has partly done this with a sketch-level model.

Not recommended, three things: general rainy-day publicity campaigns; "safety awareness" campaigns aimed at pedestrians and cyclists; and new wide arterials in the low-density periphery. The last one would copy the "wide roads are more dangerous" pattern.

Statistical models: quick reference
  • Poisson regression: ln μ = Xβ, Var = μ. Crash data are often overdispersed.
  • Negative binomial regression (NB2): Var = μ + αμ². The HSM writes μ + kμ², where k is the overdispersion parameter. As α → 0 it reduces to Poisson.
  • Pearson residual z = (y − μ̂) / √Var̂. |z| > 2 flags roughly the 95% outliers.
  • Rate ratio RR and its CI: se(ln RR) = √(1/a + 1/b).
  • Garwood exact Poisson interval: [χ²0.025(2k)/2n, χ²0.975(2k+2)/2n].
  • Log elasticity: ln μ = β0 + β1 ln x → β1 = elasticity.
  • GEH = √(2(M − C)² / (M + C)). GEH < 5 counts as a good match.

7 · Why Phase 2: the missing denominator

Phase 1 mapped 20,000 crashes. It answered "where" crashes happen, but not "why" they happen there. The reason: there were no traffic volumes. Without volumes, "many crashes" and "many vehicles" look exactly the same in the data. Phase 2 has one purpose: to supply exposure, the denominator.

The core identity

Crash cost = exposure E × risk (N / E) × severity (K / N) × unit cost (C / K) E = vehicle-kilometers traveled (VKT; VMT in US practice) or AADT. N = crash count. K = casualties. C = economic loss. Phase 1 had only N, K and C. E was always empty.

Without E, you cannot tell the two problems below apart. They call for completely different responses:

Many crashes because of many vehicles
High exposure, normal risk. The response is demand management: divert through traffic, reduce car trips, protect vulnerable road users at crossings.
Many crashes because the road is dangerous
High risk. The response is engineering: medians, access management, intersection channelization, road safety audits (RSA).

US practice is set out in Chapter 4 (network screening) of the Highway Safety Manual (HSM). It first builds a safety performance function (SPF) with AADT as exposure. An SPF predicts how many crashes a road should have, given its traffic volume. Then it screens the network with Empirical Bayes (EB). Recommendation 7 of the Phase 1 report was "build the exposure denominator and use EB for network screening". But at the time this was not possible.

8 · The four-step travel demand model

The four-step model is the standard framework for regional transportation planning at US MPOs and DOTs. It has been in use for about sixty years. The four steps answer four questions. How many trips does each zone produce and attract (trip generation)? Where do they go (trip distribution)? Which mode do they use (mode choice)? Which route do they take (traffic assignment)? Congested times from assignment feed back into distribution. The loop repeats until the results are stable.

Data and zone system

Population: ESSD 100 m grid

The census reports only at the township / subdistrict level: 25 units and 955,000 people. That is far coarser than traffic analysis zones (TAZs). Chen et al. (2024, ESSD) used ensemble learning to downscale the 7th census to a 100 m grid. The township census counts constrain the downscaling. I summed each grid cell's population into the zone that contains it. The city-wide grid total differs from the census by only 0.19%. The base year scales up to an estimated 975,000 in 2025. The 2019 year uses 99% of the 2020 grid.

TAZ design (52 internal + 14 external)

If zones are too coarse, all intrazonal trips are lost and never reach the network. If zones are too fine, the parameters cannot support them, and centroid-connector artifacts grow. The rules: cut the area into blocks along arterials, railways and rivers. No zone straddles an expressway, railway or water body. Zones have similar population. I split oversized units once more, along local streets or with population-weighted k-means. I merged the smallest units greedily into their "cheapest" neighbor. The result is 52 internal zones with a median population of 7,900. The 14 external zones are 11 road external stations, Qingshan Airport, Xichang Station and Xichang West Station. Xichang West Station opened at the end of 2022, so it is active in the base year only. About 310,000 hinterland residents live outside the study area but inside the model extent. ① Trip generation

Method

Trip generation estimates each zone's productions Pi (home end) and attractions Aj (non-home end). Productions usually come from a trip rate: population × daily trips per person × purpose share. Cross-classification is another option. Attractions come from employment or floor area × an attraction rate. For each purpose, ΣP must equal ΣA. So attractions are balanced to the production total, because home-end data are more reliable. For NHB neither end is at home, so P = A, distributed by non-residential activity.

② Trip distribution: doubly constrained gravity model

Method

Trip distribution turns Pi and Aj into an OD matrix Tij. The gravity model is an analogy to Newton's law of gravity. Trips are proportional to the size of both ends and decline with impedance cij. A doubly constrained model requires row sums = Pi and column sums = Aj:

Tij = ai · bj · Pi · Aj · f(cij)
ai = 1 / Σj bj Aj f(cij)　　bj = 1 / Σi ai Pi f(cij) a and b depend on each other. So they are solved by Furness / IPF (iterative proportional fitting). Set b = 1 and solve for a. Then use a to solve for b. Repeat until the column sums differ from A by < 1e-7.

There are three common friction (deterrence) functions: negative exponential f = e−βc, power f = c−γ, and gamma f = a·cb·ec₀c (NCHRP 716). Calibration usually targets the trip length frequency distribution (TLFD) or the mean trip length. Without a survey, only the mean can be targeted.

This project

  • Impedance cij = zone-to-zone congested car travel time (minutes). All modes share this one impedance. This is a sketch-level simplification. Intrazonal impedance = 0.5 × the time to the nearest neighboring zone. AequilibraE computes the skim matrix by shortest path. Paths may not pass through centroids.
  • f = e−βt. There is no observed TLFD. So I solved for β by bisection, so that the mean trip distance equals a target. Targets: HBW 4.5 km, HBO 3 km, NHB 3 km. The HBW target comes from GB/T 51328: in cities of 0.5–1 million people, the mean commute distance is ≤ 5 km. Note that impedance is in time but the target is in distance. That is because the standards and surveys give distances.
  • Calibrated values: βHBW = 0.0375 /min, βHBO = 0.230, βNHB = 0.220. Non-work trips are about 6 times more sensitive to time, so they are shorter.

Result: 54% of trips are shorter than 3 km, and 9% are longer than 8 km. Intrazonal trips are 7.5%. The mean HBW car time is 8.4 minutes.

Limitation: the negative exponential function fixes the "shape" of the distribution. Only the mean is constrained. Walk and bike trips use car time as impedance, which understates their cost.

③ Mode choice

Method

The standard approach is a discrete choice model (multinomial or nested logit): Pm = eVm / Σ eVk. The utility Vm combines time, cost and a mode constant. Estimating the coefficients needs a travel survey. Without a survey, the fallback is fixed mode shares or shares by distance band.

Why not logit

Xichang has no household travel survey. No utility coefficient can be estimated. Coefficients borrowed from other cities cannot have their constants calibrated either. So I used "fixed total shares + allocation by distance":

sharem(d) = cm · sm · ekm d / Σk ck sk ekk d sm is the city-wide target share (walk 28 / bike 4 / e-bike 33 / motorcycle 3 / transit 10 / car 18 / taxi 4 %). km is the utility slope per km relative to e-bike (walk −1.0, car +0.15, transit +0.08, …). The constants cm are fitted by fixed-point iteration. After fitting, the city-wide total shares equal sm exactly.

So the total shares are a scenario input. The model only decides where each mode is used. Vehicle trips = car person trips / 1.4 occupancy + taxi person trips / 1.4 × 1.3 empty-running factor. E-bike OD is output separately and not assigned to the motor-vehicle network. Transit is not assigned either.

Result: within 1 km, 73% of trips walk. From 2 to 6 km, e-bikes carry 40%. The car share is 7% within 2 km and 42% above 8 km. The e-bike share of one third matches the 33.2% in the Guilin 2017 survey. The 65% walk-and-bike share meets the GB/T 51328 target of "≥ 60% for medium-sized cities".

Limitation: mode shares are the least certain parameters in the model (car 11%–26%). Guilin is the only reference in southwest China. So I use scenarios instead of estimates.

④ Time of day and traffic assignment

Time of day

I assign only the AM peak hour. I build the AM peak OD from the daily PA matrices with departure-from-home and return-home peak factors (HBW from home 0.30, HBO from home 0.12, …). Then I scale everything so that the person-trip-weighted AM peak share = 0.15. References: Yixing 20.1% for 7–8 a.m.; the US 7.9%. Daily volume = AM peak / K. K is the ratio of network-wide AM peak VKT to daily VKT. This assumes every link has the same daily profile.

User equilibrium (UE)

Wardrop's first principle: at equilibrium, all used paths between an OD pair have equal travel time. That time is no greater than the time on any unused path. No traveler can cut their own time by switching routes alone. Beckmann showed this is equivalent to a convex optimization problem:

min Z(x) = Σa ∫0xa ta(w) dw　　s.t. Σk fkrs = qrs, f ≥ 0, xa = Σ δakrs fkrs
BPR function: ta(xa) = ta0 · [1 + α (xa / Ca)β]　　α = 0.15, β = 4 α and β are the original 1964 BPR values. NCHRP 716 Table 4.26 gives an average α of 0.42 and β of 5.2 for arterials in metro areas of 0.5–1 million. I use those as the high sensitivity case. Centroid connectors have α = 0 and β = 1, so their time does not change with volume.

t / t0 = 1 + α·(V/C)β = 1.15
So a link that takes 10 minutes at free flow now takes 11.5 minutes

Frank–Wolfe and bi-conjugate Frank–Wolfe

  1. Compute ta(xn) at the current volumes xn. Run an all-or-nothing (AON) shortest-path assignment to get the auxiliary flow yn.
  2. Line-search λ ∈ [0,1] to minimize Z(xn + λ(yn − xn)).
  3. xn+1 = xn + λ(yn − xn).

Classic FW zigzags near the optimum and converges slowly. Bi-conjugate FW (BFW) combines the descent direction with the previous two directions as conjugates. This speeds up convergence greatly. AequilibraE's bfw reaches 1e-4 in 41 iterations. The classic FW in TransCAD 4.5 ran about 300 iterations and stopped at 1.6e-4.

Relative gap

rgap = [Σa xa ta(xa) − Σrs qrs urs] / Σa xa ta(xa) The relative gap is the difference between current total travel time and the total if everyone used the current shortest path, as a share of current total time. 0 is perfect equilibrium. FHWA recommends ≤ 1e-4. It matters most when comparing alternatives. With poor convergence, convergence noise drowns out the differences between alternatives. This project reaches 8.9 × 10⁻⁵.

Feedback

The times used in distribution should equal the congested times after assignment. So I loop 3 times. Round 1: free-flow skim → gravity → mode → time of day → UE → congested skim. The congested skim is averaged with the previous round by MSA (method of successive averages), then round 2 starts. And so on. The RMSE change in the AM peak car OD falls from 4.0% in round 2 to 1.2% in round 3.

Assignment settings

There are 4 vehicle classes (multi-class): car, hinterland, ext_hub (pce 1) and ext_road (pce 1.5, many trucks). They share one volume-delay function, so the equilibrium in total pcu is unique. The algorithm is bfw with an rgap target of 1e-4.

9 · AequilibraE and TransCAD: who computed it, who replicated it

The main model runs on Python + AequilibraE. It is reproducible, free, and can batch-run the 47 sensitivity runs. TransCAD 4.5 is an independent replication engine. The question: with the same inputs in a different package, are the volumes the same? Yes, R² 0.9999. So the assignment results do not depend on one software implementation.

What AequilibraE is

AequilibraE is an open-source Python library for travel demand modeling. It has four main parts: network / graph (Graph) building; shortest paths and skim matrices (NetworkSkimming); multi-class static traffic assignment (all-or-nothing, MSA, Frank–Wolfe, conjugate FW, bi-conjugate FW); and BPR-type volume-delay functions, select-link analysis and the OMX matrix format. It plays the role of the "assignment engine" in TransCAD / Visum / Cube. It has no GUI and no built-in gravity model or mode choice. I wrote those two steps myself in numpy.

Role in this project

  • Build an AequilibraE project from links.gpkg. One each for the base year, 2019 and 2035.
  • Skims use Graph. set_skimming(["time","distance"]) gets time and distance. set_blocked_centroid_flows(True) stops paths from passing through centroids.
  • Assignment: one TrafficClass per vehicle class. set_vdf("BPR") reads per-link α / β fields. set_algorithm("bfw"), with an rgap target of 1e-4.
  • Select-link analysis gives intersection turning movements. VISSIM uses these volumes.

Environment note: AequilibraE needs pandas 3. That conflicts with the geopandas environment. So I keep it in its own venv. The pipeline switches between the two interpreters.

TransCAD basics

  • TransCAD is Caliper's GIS-based transportation planning package. It is one of the main tools at US MPOs, alongside Cube, Visum and EMME.
  • Data structures: a geographic file (.dbd) holds a line layer and a node layer. Line endpoints automatically form the node layer. A network (.net) is built from the line layer. It holds direction fields (AB / BA field pairs match automatically), length, time and capacity. A matrix (.mtx) holds several cores and indices.
  • Centroids: Network Settings defines a selection set of centroid nodes. Paths may not pass through centroids. This is equivalent to blocked centroid flows in AequilibraE. Without this setting, results differ.
  • Menu path: Networks/Paths → Create / Settings / Multiple Paths gives the skim matrix. Planning → Trip Distribution → Gravity Evaluation, doubly constrained, with Exponential / Gamma / Power impedance. Planning → Traffic Assignment, Method = User Equilibrium, with Time field, Capacity field, Alpha / Beta, Iterations and Convergence.
  • GISDK: TransCAD's scripting language (Caliper Script). RSCC.EXE compiles macros into a UI database. tcw.exe -a <ui> -ai "<macro>" launches it from the command line. Batch procedures are called with RunMacro("TCB Run Procedure", …). They use the same code as the menu commands.

10 · Checking a model without counts: reasonableness, 47 sensitivity runs, replication

How do you check a model with no traffic counts? I use three layers. Reasonableness: do the intermediate quantities fall within ranges from comparable cities and standards? Sensitivity: 47 full runs test whether the conclusions are robust. Replication: an independent re-run in TransCAD. One point matters: this is not validation.

Three concepts from the FHWA manual

The Travel Model Validation and Reasonableness Checking Manual, 2nd ed. (Cambridge Systematics for FHWA / TMIP, 2010) separates three concepts:

Validation
Compares model output with independent observations. These include screenline and cordon counts, travel surveys and travel times. Metrics include %RMSE by volume group, GEH, VMT by facility class vs HPMS, and screenline error within ±10%.
Reasonableness
Checks whether intermediate quantities and results fall in plausible ranges. The ranges come from comparable cities, standards and experience. Items include trip rates per person, purpose split, average trip length, intrazonal share, mode shares, VMT per person, V/C distribution and convergence.
Sensitivity
Changes the parameters. Checks whether the model responds sensibly and the conclusions hold.

Xichang has no counts and no surveys. So this project does only the last two. I do not call it validation.

ρ = 1 − 6 Σ di² / ( n (n² − 1) ) di is the difference in a link's rank between two runs. I compared each run's daily link volumes with the base run. Median 0.988, minimum 0.90. The parameters mostly scale all volumes up or down together. The relative order stays stable.

Results

  • The largest effects on total VKT: external volume −15% / +19%, car share −15% / +16%, hinterland city-bound share ±12%. Trip rate and trip length come next. Under LHS, the 5–95% range of total VKT is 2.59–3.71 million (−19% / +16%). Two of the top three parameters concern traffic from outside the study area. That is exactly where the data are weakest.
  • The largest effects on congestion (share of links with V/C > 1): capacity (13.8%–2.4%) and peak-hour share. They barely change VKT. BPR parameters and through-traffic share have small effects on both.
  • Rank stability: of the 25 busiest links in the base run, 14 stay in the top 20 in ≥ 80% of runs. Of the top 15 corridors, 14 stay in the top 15 in ≥ 80% of runs.
  • Policy implication: the best way to narrow exposure uncertainty is classified counts. Place them at the entry points and the main corridors into the city. Together they form a cordon line around the study area. They constrain external volume, hinterland share and car share at once.

11 · Exposure adjustment: SPF, Empirical Bayes, and how the conclusion flipped

I have 2019 vehicle-kilometers traveled (VKT) for every link. With it, I can apply the standard HSM steps. Step 1: fit a safety performance function (SPF) for each road class. The SPF predicts how many crashes a road of that type "should" have, given its traffic. Step 2: use Empirical Bayes (EB) to weight the observed and predicted counts. This gives the excess crashes (PSI). Step 3: answer one question again. Are through arterials truly dangerous, or do they just carry more traffic?

I snapped 9,384 angle, rear-end and rollover crash points to the 2019 model network. I used three rules. Links within 30 m are candidates. Empirical Bayes and PSI

Principle (Hauer 1997; HSM Chapter 4): observed crash counts fluctuate at random. They also show regression to the mean: a site with many crashes one year tends to have fewer the next. EB weights the observed count and the SPF prediction by their reliability:

w = 1 / (1 + k · μ)
NEB = w · μ + (1 − w) · Nobs
Excess (PSI, potential for safety improvement) = NEB − μ Larger μ or larger k gives a smaller w. A smaller w puts more trust in the observed count. PSI answers one question: "How many more crashes does this road have than roads of the same class and traffic?" Engineering treatments can reduce exactly this part. HSM network screening recommends ranking by EB excess, not by crash count or crash rate.

Scenario setup

Three population levels

Central city of 600,000 / 700,000 / 850,000 residents. The 850,000 figure: development boundary of 85.79 km² ÷ 100 m² per person. Today's population is 470,000. So growth is 130,000 / 230,000 / 380,000 residents.

Allocation rule: "water-filling"

The four-step model is a static macroscopic model. It cannot see signals, queues or left-turners forcing into gaps. So I drilled down to one intersection. VISSIM simulates each vehicle every 0.1 seconds. FHWA's SSAM then counts near misses, or conflicts, from the trajectories. This section first covers what is simulated and why, then how I built it.

Why Hangtian Avenue × Shengli South Road

This card explains the choice. The site meets three conditions at once:

  1. It ranks first among intersections in Phase 1, with 77 crashes: 42 angle and 33 rear-end. The second-ranked site has only 36.
  2. Exposure does not explain it away. Its south-east approach link has an EB excess of +70 crashes. That is about 13 times the SPF prediction. It ranks 6th among 640 links with volumes. Yet the Hangtian Avenue corridor as a whole has a negative excess. So the risk is concentrated at this node, not along the corridor.
  3. It is an at-grade signalized intersection in the central city. Engineers can act on it.

This closes the "macro → meso → micro" chain. The demand model says Hangtian Avenue as a whole just carries a lot of traffic. EB says: except at this intersection. VISSIM answers: how can this intersection be changed?

How VISSIM works

Microsimulation

Microsimulation models each vehicle at each time step, here 10 steps per second. It models each vehicle's acceleration, deceleration, lane changes and yielding. It can show bottlenecks such as signals, queues and weaving. A static macroscopic model cannot see these. So the two complement each other.

Wiedemann car-following model (psycho-physical model)

A car-following model describes how a driver follows the vehicle ahead. The driver switches among four states based on distance and speed difference: free driving, approaching, following and braking. Drivers cannot hold an exact distance. They drift between perception thresholds. This is called car-following oscillation.

  • Wiedemann 74 (urban): three parameters. ax is the average standstill distance, default 2 m. bx_add is the additive safety distance, set to 2. bx_mult is the multiplicative part, set to 3. Desired safety distance d = ax + (bx_add + bx_mult · z) · √v. These three parameters mainly set the saturation flow rate. You can tune them to 1,600–1,800 pcu/h/lane.
  • Wiedemann 99 (freeway / interurban): 10 parameters, more detailed. For example, CC0 is standstill distance, CC1 is headway time, CC2 is following variation.
  • In this project, motor vehicles use W74 "Urban (motorized)". E-bike links use "Cycle-Track (free overtaking)," which allows side-by-side passing.

Lane changes

There are two kinds. A necessary lane change follows the route. A free lane change seeks a faster lane. Parameters: maximum deceleration, accepted deceleration, waiting time, and safety distance factors front and rear. Waiting time defaults to 60 s. After that, the vehicle is removed from the network. This is why 17 vehicles were removed under the existing plan.

Signals, seeds, evaluation

  • Fixed-time signals: a VisSig .sig file defines signal groups, phases, cycle length and green splits. Signal heads sit at the stop line of each lane. The student edition cannot connect external actuated control.
  • Random seeds: arrivals, desired speeds and driver parameters are all drawn at random. So one run is only one sample. This project runs 20 seeds per plan. I use seeds as samples in the statistical tests. The first 120 s are warm-up and are not counted.
  • Evaluation outputs: node evaluation gives volume, average delay, stops and queues for each turning movement. There are also travel-time sections, delay segments and data collection points.
  • Webster timing: cycle C0 = (1.5 L + 5) / (1 − Y). Y = Σ yi, the sum of critical lane flow ratios q/s for each phase. L is total lost time. Effective green gi = (C − L) · yi / Y. This project uses a saturation flow of 1,700 pcu/h/lane and 4 s lost time per phase.

SSAM: near misses instead of crashes

SSAM is the Surrogate Safety Assessment Model. FHWA developed it (Gettman & Head 2008). It reads the microsimulation trajectory file (.trj), finds conflict events between vehicle pairs, and computes surrogate safety measures. The logic: crashes are rare, and simulated vehicles do not crash. So near misses stand in for crashes.

  • TTC (time to collision): assume both vehicles keep their current speed and heading. TTC is the time until they collide. A minimum TTC at or below a threshold counts as a conflict. The default threshold is 1.5 s.
  • PET (post-encroachment time): the time between the first vehicle leaving the conflict point and the second vehicle arriving there. Threshold ≤ 5 s.
  • Other severity measures include MaxS, DeltaS, DR and MaxDeltaV.
  • Classification by conflict angle: based on the angle between the two headings. < 30° is rear-end. 30–85° is lane-change. > 85° is crossing. The SSAM default crossing threshold is 80°. This project uses 85°.
  • Standard cleaning: remove records with TTC = 0. These are simulation artifacts where vehicle outlines already overlap. They make up 84–88% of records. Remove the warm-up period. Normalize per 1,000 entering vehicles.

SSAM conflicts are not crashes. Their use here: check whether the conflict types a plan reduces match the main crash types at this intersection.

The build

Software: VISSIM 5.30 student edition. Limits: network ≤ 1 km × 1 km, ≤ 600 s per run, ≤ 10 signal controllers, no pedestrians, no COM interface, and a watermark. Without COM, Python cannot drive VISSIM. But the .inp file is plain text. So a script generates the entire network.

Geometry comes from OSM and imagery. The intersection is a skewed four-leg junction. Hangtian Avenue is a divided road with 2 lanes per direction and frontage roads on both sides. All four corners have channelized right turns. Lanes at the stop line: Hangtian Avenue 2 through + 2 left; Shengli South Road 2 through + 1 left. Left-turn bays are 70 / 50 m. E-bike lanes run outside the motor lanes. The script generates links, connectors, vehicle inputs, routing decisions, reduced-speed areas, signal controllers and heads, nodes, delay segments, queue counters, conflict areas, Multirun settings and the background map.

E-bike OD was loaded separately. The model loads the approaches to V/C 1.2–1.3. Turning volume is 10,461 veh/h, beyond what any signal timing can serve. 89 conflict areas written by script. The network has 117 overlaps. I set priority on 89. Type 1 is conflicts between streams that share a green: 45 areas. Rule: through > left turn > channelized right turn; turning vehicles yield to through e-bikes. Type 2 is signal-separated conflicts involving a left turn: 44 areas. Permissive left-turners wait inside the intersection for a gap. They often clear during the yellow or all-red. By then the cross traffic has started. If these conflict areas stay passive, starting vehicles drive through the clearing left-turners. The intersection's main crash risk would then go unmeasured. So I set clearing left-turners have priority; starting cross traffic yields. Gaps: permissive left turns use a front gap of 1.0 s and a rear gap of 2.0 s. Run protocol: 600 s per run. The first 120 s are warm-up; analysis covers 120–600 s. 20 seeds per plan. Output is node evaluation. Trajectories go to SSAM 3 with TTC 1.5, PET 5, crossing 85°.
